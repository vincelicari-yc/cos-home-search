# Value Markers & Livability

Mostly from `[T07]` and `[T08]` — the two transcripts most applicable to our search, because
they're about evaluating any individual house rather than recommending neighborhoods.

`[T07]`'s framing: two homes, same square footage, same bedrooms, same lot size, three streets
apart — **$900,000 and $750,000.** The data most people look at isn't what drives the price.

> **Avoid "Excel spreadsheet syndrome."** `[T07]` Obsessing over price per square foot and how
> new the quartz countertops are. Those matter, but: **"You can buy a $50,000 kitchen renovation,
> but you cannot buy a 40-year-old blue spruce in your backyard or move a mountain to get a
> better view."**

That's the organizing principle for this file. **Weight the things that can't be changed.**

---

## Can't be changed — weight these heavily

### Sun and orientation
The most concrete, most overlooked item in the batch, and it's a daily-life issue rather than an
abstraction. Colorado Springs gets ~300 days of sunshine `[T07]`.

- **A south-facing driveway means snow melts by noon. Across the street, the neighbor is still
  chipping ice three days later.** `[T07]` `[BASE]`
- **A west-facing deck** gets sunset views over Pikes Peak — but **without a covered patio you're
  cooked in 90° July heat.** Orientation determines what you'll actually *do* with outdoor space
  versus what you imagine doing when you first see it `[T07]`
- At 6,000+ ft (7,500 on the northeast side) the UV load is **"just cooking everything"** on the
  exterior `[T08]` — south and west exposures punish paint, decks, seals, and shingles
- East-facing living space gets the mountain morning light `[BASE]`

**On a tour:** note which way the driveway faces, which rooms take afternoon west sun, and whether
any deck or patio is covered. On a sloped north-facing driveway, ice is a months-long problem.

### Views, and whether they're protected
Mountain and Pikes Peak views carry real premium `[T07]` `[T03]`. Ask whether a future build
could take them `[BASE]`.

### Mature trees — an asset and a liability at once
> **Disputed across transcripts, and both sides are right.**
>
> `[T07]`: **"Neighborhoods with a canopy always carry a premium."** You can't fast-forward 30
> years of tree growth. You *can* transplant large trees, but at a serious price tag. Mature
> landscaping is a genuine, unbuyable value marker.
>
> `[T04]`: insurers and lenders **may require cutting back the big privacy trees**, which are also
> **"a huge issue even to your foundation."** `[T03]` stresses keeping trees trimmed and removing
> dead ones as fire mitigation. `[BASE]`: no large trees within ~15 ft of the foundation.
> `[T03]` also notes mature trees mean **higher insurance** in Summerfield.
>
> **Our resolution:** mature canopy *in the neighborhood and away from the structure* is an asset
> worth paying for. Mature trees **overhanging the roof, within ~15 ft of the foundation, or dead
> and unmitigated** are a liability and a negotiation item. Judge tree *placement*, not tree
> presence.

### Lot position
- **Be very cautious buying a house at the bottom of a hill** `[T04]`
- Backing to open space is a plus `[T03]`, but at the foothills interface it's also wildfire
  exposure `[T04]` — see `20-deal-breakers.md`
- Backyard privacy and sight lines `[BASE]`; note that luxury west-side areas often **won't allow
  6-ft privacy fences** `[T03]`
- Quiet street vs. arterial noise — stand outside and just listen for a minute `[BASE]`

### Neighborhood build-out — the appreciation lever
The `[T08]` thesis, covered in full in `30-our-radius-neighborhoods.md`: **buy where they can't
build more.** New construction caps resale appreciation for its area. Our built-out west-central
radius is structurally favored. He also likes neighborhoods **surrounded by already-developed
areas** with good geography, such as a large city park nearby.

## Can be changed — but price it before the offer

### Layout, storage, and flex space
`[T08]` flags this as genuinely underrated, and specifically Coloradan:

- **Storage.** People here have outdoor gear. "You might be in it for a year, maybe two, and then
  realize you don't have room to put any of your stuff." `[T08]`
- **A mud room or transition space** from outdoors before entering the house `[T08]`
- **Flex space** for cold winter months and working from home `[T08]`
- Think about the **next buyer's** gear and needs too, not just ours `[T08]`

For us specifically: 4 bed / 3 bath is the hard minimum, but check that a basement bedroom has a
**proper egress window** `[BASE]`, that all three baths are usefully placed rather than stranded,
and that there's room to host people.

### Durability over finishes
`[T08]`'s rules, verbatim in spirit:
- **Limited future supply beats new**
- **Proven durability beats trendy finishes**
- **Livable layouts beat Instagram trends**
- **Payments that work today beat risky appreciation bets**

Check the exterior materials, because that's where our climate does its damage: **shingle type
drastically changes the insurance premium**; badly faded paint means at minimum deferred
maintenance; **for stucco, look for hail pock marks and cracking** `[T08]`.

### Renovation costs are higher here than buyers expect
- Kitchen remodel averages **~$25,000** in Colorado; full professional remodel up to **$60/sqft**
  `[T06]`
- **Higher in older neighborhoods** because of older building systems and hidden structural
  surprises `[T06]`
- **"A home listed at [$450k] that needs $80,000 of work is not a [$450k] home."** `[T06]`
- Expect sellers in older established areas to be **retired, long-tenured, and unwilling to
  renovate** `[T03]` — so assume we inherit the deferred maintenance

## Fit — the thing no rubric scores

`[T07]` is direct about this, and it's worth keeping in view while we score everything else:

> "Two homes can look identical on a screen but live completely different. And that feeling you
> get when you pull into a driveway, that's what people are actually paying for."
>
> "The best value isn't always the lowest price per square foot or the shiniest backsplash.
> **It's the home that fits how you actually spend your days.**"

The west side is older with **genuinely funky floor plans**, but feels like a mountain town —
long-standing coffee shops, quick access to some of the best Front Range trails `[T07]`. Planned
communities out east are polished and amenity-rich. **Neither is better; the value is in which
one matches your actual life.** Don't buy modern if you love historic, and don't buy a project
house if what you want is move-in ready `[T07]`.

Given the anchor is YWAM and Kairos Coffee, the west-side character is probably the closer fit —
but that's a judgment for the family, not the rubric.
