⚠️ Do these two things before touring any house

Either one can rule out a house on its own, and both change what we can afford to borrow — so they come before falling in love with a place, not after.

  1. Get an insurance quote for that exact address. In wildfire zones near the foothills, premiums run $4,500–$8,500 a year — that's $300–600 a month on top of the mortgage. Some insurers won't write these addresses at all. The Zillow monthly estimate does not include this.
  2. Pull the FEMA flood map. Monument Creek runs right through our search area. Flood insurance adds $1,000–$5,000 a year, and a flood-zone home can appraise lower, which complicates the loan.

What we've learned so far

From eight videos on the Colorado Springs market. The full write-up lives in the criteria/ folder in the repo.

Mountain Shadows is close, and the riskiest thing in our radius

It's 6.6 minutes away with mountain views, so it's tempting. But 100% of properties there carry wildfire risk over the next 30 years, and 23% carry severe flood risk on top of it — the 2012 Waldo Canyon fire removed the vegetation that used to absorb runoff. Insurance has gone from $1,800–2,800 a year to $4,500–8,500. The median sale price is about $620,000, which is over our ceiling before insurance is even counted. Not ruled out — but get the quote first.

Rockrimmon, Peregrine, and Cedar Heights are out

All three came up in the videos as places to consider, and all three fail our 10-minute rule once actually measured: 11.9, 11.2, and 16.7 minutes. Peregrine also starts in the high $600s. Cedar Heights' gated, winding approach costs far more time than its 7 miles suggests.

Vista Grande may be the best value in the city right now

It's 3.3 minutes from YWAM, and its zip code (80918) was named as having the most price cuts, the most days on market, and the most negotiating room of anywhere in Colorado Springs. The tradeoff is slower near-term appreciation — which matters less for a home we plan to live in. Worth watching closely.

Our area is on the right side of the market shift

The strongest claim in the batch: if you buy where builders can easily put up more homes, new construction caps how much your resale can appreciate. The advice was to buy in built-out west-side neighborhoods — which is exactly our whole search radius. Nothing new is being built here to compete with us.

Ask for a Class 4 roof, not just "a new roof"

Colorado Springs gets 5.5 hail days a year, and hail is 60–70% of all Colorado insurance claims. A Class 4 impact-resistant roof cuts the insurance premium 20–30% every year we own the house. Also worth knowing: insurers are shifting older roofs to "actual cash value," meaning an aging roof may not be covered for full replacement.

A repaired foundation is a good sign, not a red flag

About 25% of Colorado Springs homes have a foundation issue at some point — the clay soil here swells up to 20% in volume when wet. The best buy in a soil-risk area is one where the work is already done, documented, and warrantied. Ask whether the warranty transfers.

Asking prices are running about $79k above reality

In early 2026 the average listing price was $618,000 while the average home actually sold for $540,000. Sellers are pricing at 2022 levels and buyers are paying 2026 prices. Offering under list is normal here right now, not insulting — and contract fallout is at an all-time high because sellers won't fix things.

Check who sends the water bill

Small question, big number. If Colorado Springs Utilities covers all four services on one bill, rates are among the most competitive in the state. A private water co-op, or services split across providers, can add hundreds of dollars a month. Same for metro districts — they aren't an HOA, don't show up as dues, and quietly raise the tax bill.
